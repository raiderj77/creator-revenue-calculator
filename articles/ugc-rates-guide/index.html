UGC Rates: How to Scope and Price a Creator Project

There is no single UGC rate that fits every project. Build a defensible quote by defining the deliverables, choosing your own creation fee, and itemizing production costs, revisions, raw files, usage permissions, exclusivity, and rush work.

A UGC quote is not the same as a universal rate

“UGC rate” can describe several different things: the fee to create content, the price of extra versions or raw files, permission for a brand to reuse the work, or the creator's income across many completed projects. Combining those questions into one benchmark hides the scope that actually changes the quote.

A quote should answer a narrower question: what would you charge for this defined project under these written terms? The free worksheet does the arithmetic after you supply those terms. It does not choose a market rate, predict whether a brand will accept the quote, or estimate monthly earnings.

If the creator must post to their own audience: treat that distribution requirement as a separate sponsorship scope. The sponsorship quote worksheet keeps audience distribution distinct from content production.

Define the scope before choosing a price

Start with the brief and turn every vague request into a line item. This makes the quote easier to review and reduces the chance that two parties attach different meanings to the same total.

Scope areaQuestions to answer in writingWhy it belongs in the quote
DeliverablesWhich formats, lengths, aspect ratios, hooks, captions, and file types are required?Defines what the base creation fee covers.
ProductionWho supplies the product, location, props, talent, travel, shipping, and specialized equipment?Separates project costs from creator compensation.
RevisionsWhat is included, who approves it, and what counts as a new concept rather than a revision?Makes later changes visible instead of silently expanding scope.
Files and variantsDoes the brand need raw footage, alternate hooks, crops, captions, or editable project files?Distinguishes final deliverables from reusable production assets.
UsageWhere, for what purpose, in which territory, and for what dates may the content be used?Turns a broad request for “rights” into reviewable permission terms.
ExclusivityWhich named competitors or categories are restricted, in which places, and for what dates?Shows the business opportunities the restriction may limit.
TimelineWhen are the brief, product, draft, feedback, approval, and final files due?Identifies whether shortened deadlines require separate planning.

Build the quote from visible inputs

Choose the base creation fee from your own production time, direct costs, records of prior accepted work, and business requirements. Then enter each add-on separately instead of burying permissions or extra work inside one unexplained number.

(base creation fee × deliverables) + production costs + revisions + raw footage or variants + usage rights + exclusivity + rush = quote total

Synthetic arithmetic example, not a benchmark: a $120 creation fee × 2 deliverables, plus $35 production, $25 revisions, $40 raw footage or variants, $80 usage rights, $60 exclusivity, and $20 rush equals a $500 quote scenario. These invented values demonstrate the formula only; they are not suggested UGC rates.

The quote total still excludes every item you leave out, including taxes, payment processing, insurance, shipping, travel, licensing, professional review, and collection risk. Add relevant exclusions and payment terms to the written proposal rather than assuming the calculator covers them.

Keep production, permission, and ownership terms separate

The U.S. Copyright Office's Copyright Basics circular explains that copyright generally begins with the author, that owning a copy of a work is not the same as owning its copyright, and that a transfer of copyright ownership generally must be written and signed. That is why a brief request for “full rights” is not specific enough for a pricing decision.

Write down the allowed channels, paid or organic use, territory, start and end dates, editing permissions, sublicensing, and renewal process. If the client wants ownership rather than limited permission, label that request distinctly and obtain qualified legal review when the consequences are material.

Do not assume every commissioned creator project is automatically a work made for hire. The Copyright Office's Works Made for Hire circular says the classification depends on the working relationship or, for certain commissioned works, specific statutory categories and an express signed agreement. The circular also warns that the classification has serious consequences for authorship and ownership.

Include disclosure requirements in the brief

If the creator endorses or recommends the product, the FTC's Disclosures 101 guidance says a material connection to the brand should be disclosed clearly, in a place people can see and understand, with the endorsement itself. For video, the FTC says the disclosure should appear in the video rather than only in its description.

Before quoting, clarify whether the content includes an endorsement, who reviews disclosure language, where the disclosure must appear, and whether alternate versions require the disclosure to be rebuilt. This is a production requirement, not a promise that any particular wording will satisfy every law or platform policy.

How much do UGC creators make?

A single quote cannot answer that question. Creator income depends on which quotes are accepted, which projects are completed and paid, the creator's direct and operating costs, and the time period being measured. A proposed quote is not revenue, and gross collected revenue is not the same as take-home income.

After a project is paid, record the actual amount and known costs in the blank Creator Revenue Tracker. Keep private client details in your own secure records; the public calculator does not need names, contracts, or campaign files.

Before sending the quote

  1. Confirm the deliverable list, technical specifications, and review process.
  2. Separate creator-audience distribution from content production.
  3. State what the base fee includes and itemize every selected add-on.
  4. Define usage, ownership, raw-file, editing, and exclusivity terms in writing.
  5. Record deadlines, payment terms, cancellation terms, and exclusions.
  6. Check required endorsement disclosures and seek qualified advice for unclear legal or tax terms.
